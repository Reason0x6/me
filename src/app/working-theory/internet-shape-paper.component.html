What Shape Is
the Internet?

Reconstructing network geometry across physical, topological, latency, and temporal dimensions

AuthorGavin Austin
PublicationWorking Theory
StatusProposed measurement programme
RightsAll rights reserved
00

Abstract

The Internet is commonly drawn on a geographic map, even though geography is only one of several structures that determine how two connected machines experience one another.

This paper proposes treating the Internet as an unknown geometric object reconstructed from measurements. A set of globally distributed nodes measures pairwise round-trip latency, path traces, reachability, and temporal variation. Those observations are joined with geography, Autonomous System routing, Internet exchange, and facility metadata. Multiple reconstruction methods—multidimensional scaling, network coordinates, graph and hyperbolic embeddings, shortest-path inference, clustering, network tomography, persistent homology, anomaly detection, and temporal statistics—then produce competing descriptions of the same object.

The central result is not expected to be a single definitive map. Geographic distance is approximately symmetric and spherical; routing is directed and constrained by policy; latency can violate triangle inequality; topology is hierarchical and incomplete; time changes every layer. The appropriate output is therefore an atlas of Internet-spaces, accompanied by distortion, uncertainty, stability, and falsification tests. This document specifies the questions, measurement design, mathematical objects, reconstruction pipeline, visualisations, and reproducibility requirements for building that atlas without mistaking an attractive projection for ground truth.

Keywords Internet measurement · network geometry · latency · topology · multidimensional scaling · network tomography · hyperbolic embedding · temporal networks
Research status

This is a pre-registered research design and visualisation specification. It contains no fabricated measurements or empirical claims about particular city pairs. Sydney–Singapore and other city relationships are used only as testable illustrations until the measurement campaign is executed.

01

The problem

The object behind the map

A conventional Internet map begins with a world map and draws network observations on top. This proposal reverses the order: begin with observations and ask what kind of space could have produced them.

Physical infrastructure is geographic. Fibre occupies routes on land and seabed; signals propagate at a finite speed; landing stations and data centres have coordinates. Yet packets do not select routes by geographic length. Inter-domain routing expresses reachability, commercial relationships, local policy, resilience, and traffic engineering. A packet can leave a country, cross an exchange elsewhere, and return. A nearby network can be many administrative boundaries away, while a distant network can be one well-provisioned interconnection away.

Latency is a third object. It includes propagation, serialization, switching, queueing, path inflation, access-network behaviour, and measurement noise. Round-trip delay is usually asymmetric in cause even when recorded as one symmetric-looking number. Internet delay also exhibits triangle-inequality violations: an indirect overlay route through a third endpoint can be faster than the default direct route because the paths selected by routing policy are not chosen to minimise delay [8]. A Euclidean map must distort such observations because Euclidean distances cannot behave that way.

The Internet therefore has several shapes at once. The physical shape constrains the lower bound. The topological shape describes attachment and reachability. The policy shape determines which paths are available and preferred. The latency shape records the experienced cost of those choices. The temporal shape describes how all of the above move. The research object is not any one layer, but the residual structure between them.

02

Research frame

Questions and hypotheses

The primary question is: what families of geometric and topological models reconstruct measured Internet relationships with the least distortion, and how does that answer change with scale, protocol, location, and time?

The language of “shape” is operational rather than metaphorical. A shape is a representation that preserves specified relations—near-neighbour order, measured distance, path adjacency, hierarchy, cycles, or temporal continuity—within quantified error. Different representations can be valid for different relations.

H1

Geography sets a floor, not a map

Great-circle distance will explain a meaningful share of minimum RTT, but residuals will remain structured by cable routes, interconnection, access networks, and policy.

H2

Two dimensions are descriptive, not sufficient

A 2D Euclidean embedding will expose broad regions but produce unstable neighbourhoods and concentrated distortion around inter-domain boundaries.

H3

The shape is not fully metric

Asymmetry and triangle-inequality violations will persist after robust aggregation, indicating that a directed quasi-metric or graph is more faithful than a single symmetric Euclidean space.

H4

Topology prefers negative curvature

Hyperbolic embeddings will preserve AS hierarchy and greedy path structure better than equal-dimensional Euclidean embeddings, consistent with prior AS-level mapping [10].

H5

Shape changes through events

Cable faults, route leaks, peering changes, congestion, and maintenance will appear as coherent motion or tearing in latent space rather than independent noisy links.

H6

There is no globally optimal dimension

The dimension that predicts unseen latencies will vary by region and network class; the atlas should report local intrinsic dimension instead of one global number.

03

Protocol

Measurement design

3.1 Observation nodes

The initial campaign uses 240–320 stable RIPE Atlas probes. RIPE Atlas exposes globally distributed volunteer-hosted probes and supports user-defined ping and traceroute measurements [1]. Sampling must be stratified by continent, subregion, country, Autonomous System, access type, and IPv4/IPv6 capability. No country contributes more than a fixed share of the primary panel, and no ASN contributes more than two probes unless it is the explicit object of a within-network experiment.

Geographic diversity without topological diversity is insufficient. Ten probes in one city but attached to ten networks may reveal more than ten probes in ten cities behind one provider. Selection therefore maximises a joint coverage objective over geography and ASN while reserving a smaller residential panel to prevent university and data-centre networks from defining the entire inferred shape.

3.2 A sparse, repeated measurement schedule

A complete directed matrix for n nodes requires n(n−1) measurements per round. At 300 nodes that is 89,700 directed pairs before repetition, expensive both in measurement credits and network traffic. The campaign uses a landmark-plus-rotation design: 24 topologically and geographically diverse landmarks are measured by every node; every node also measures a rotating sample of ordinary peers; a 60-node validation panel performs dense measurements during bounded windows.

Each scheduled observation sends a small train of probes rather than relying on one packet. Ping and Paris-style traceroute are collected in paired windows. Measurements repeat every 15 minutes for eight weeks, with denser five-minute windows around declared experiments. IPv4 and IPv6 remain separate datasets until comparison. CAIDA Ark and its long-running traceroute topology datasets provide an independent macroscopic view and historical context [2][3].

3.3 Robust latency matrices

Queueing makes the mean RTT a poor proxy for the underlying path floor. For directed pair i→j in time window t, the baseline uses a low quantile after removing failed probes and structurally invalid results. The median remains a parallel “experienced latency” measure; their difference is an estimate of variable delay, not something to silently discard.

L→ij(t) = Q0.05 { RTT(k)i→j(t) : k ∈ valid probes }
Eq. 1

Low-quantile directed latency approximates the path floor while retaining a separate distribution for congestion and jitter.

DLij = ½(L→ij + L→ji)    ;    Aij = (L→ij − L→ji) / DLij
Eq. 2

The symmetric matrix supports conventional embedding. The asymmetry matrix is preserved as a first-class object rather than averaged out of the study.

3.4 Provenance and enrichment

Every observation carries probe identity, source and destination ASN, protocol family, timestamp, packet size, firmware version where available, and measurement identifier. Traceroute hops are resolved conservatively into router and AS paths; unresolved and non-responsive hops remain explicit. PeeringDB supplies self-reported networks, exchanges, and facility presence [13]. These records explain possible interconnection but are not treated as proof that a measured packet used a particular facility.

Submarine-cable and terrestrial-route data are similarly explanatory priors, never forced labels. Inference must be allowed to contradict metadata: a route may traverse an undocumented interconnection, an IP may be mis-geolocated, and two interfaces may belong to one router. The dataset records source, retrieval date, confidence, and licence for every enrichment field.

04

Mathematical objects

Three distances, several geometries

4.1 Geographic distance

For endpoints with latitude and longitude, DG is the WGS84 geodesic or, for exploratory work, the great-circle distance. Geography supplies a hard conceptual lower bound, though endpoint coordinates, conduit routes, slack loops, and optical equipment keep it from being an exact physical path length.

Tfloorij = 2DGij / vfibre    ;    ρij = DLij / Tfloorij
Eq. 3

With vfibre stated as an assumption, ρ measures end-to-end inflation above an idealised great-circle fibre RTT. It mixes path stretch and equipment delay and must not be interpreted as route length alone [11].

4.2 Network distance

There is no scalar network distance that preserves all relevant structure. The study retains a vector: router-hop count, AS-hop count, inferred relationship sequence, number of exchange crossings, and shortest-path distance in reconstructed IP- and AS-level graphs. Rocketfuel demonstrated how targeted traceroute, routing tables, alias resolution, and DNS information can recover ISP structure more efficiently than naïve all-to-all mapping [4]. The present study uses that lesson while keeping uncertainty around every inferred node and edge.

A policy-weighted graph assigns different costs to customer-provider, peer, sibling, and uncertain edges. Directed shortest paths then represent reachability under an explicit policy model. They are not claimed to reproduce BGP selection exactly; rather, disagreement between predicted and observed paths becomes a diagnostic.

4.3 Latency distance

The baseline matrix DL can be embedded, but it is not assumed to be Euclidean. Global Network Positioning and Vivaldi showed that synthetic coordinates can summarise end-to-end latency efficiently [5][6]. Later deployments found substantial triangle-inequality violations and dataset-dependent intrinsic dimensionality [7][9]. These are not inconvenient outliers; they are evidence about the object.

The project therefore publishes four related latency objects: a symmetric low-quantile distance, a directed latency matrix, a distributional distance comparing full RTT distributions, and a multiplex graph whose layers encode baseline delay, jitter, loss, and reachability. Methods receive the object whose assumptions they satisfy.

Inference

Reconstruction pipeline

5.1 Weighted multidimensional scaling

Classical MDS provides a spectral baseline for dense symmetric matrices. Weighted metric and non-metric MDS extend the analysis to missing pairs and uncertain observations. For dimension d, coordinates xi ∈ ℝd minimise normalised stress. Weights combine sample count, measurement stability, and pair-level confidence.

Stressd(X) = [ Σi<j wij( ‖xi−xj‖ − DLij )² / Σi<j wij(DLij)² ]½
Eq. 4

Stress is reported globally, by region, by ASN class, and by distance band. A low global number cannot conceal concentrated failure for under-sampled networks.

A

Coordinate models

Classical, metric, non-metric, landmark, and robust MDS; GNP-style landmark coordinates; Vivaldi-style online coordinates. Compare Euclidean, spherical, and product spaces.

B

Graph reconstruction

Build router- and AS-level graphs from traceroutes, resolve aliases conservatively, infer shortest and near-shortest paths, and preserve directed policy annotations.

C

Graph embeddings

Fit spectral, Laplacian, diffusion, and random-walk embeddings. Measure whether local neighbourhoods, communities, and observed paths survive projection.

D

Hyperbolic models

Fit Poincaré and likelihood-based AS embeddings. Compare greedy-routing success, path stretch, link prediction, hierarchy, and stability with Euclidean baselines.

E

Topology reconstruction

Construct Vietoris–Rips filtrations from distance thresholds. Track components, loops, and void-like features across scale rather than selecting one arbitrary edge cutoff.

F

Temporal models

Align successive embeddings, estimate drift and changepoints, and separate global events from endpoint noise using robust covariance and graph-coherent residuals.

5.2 Shortest-path inference and residual explanation

For each observed pair, models predict latency from geography alone, topology alone, and a combined feature set. Nested models make the comparison interpretable: geographic distance first; then cable and facility features; then AS and peering structure; then time-varying congestion features. Out-of-sample improvement estimates how much structure each layer contributes.

Residuals are joined back to traceroute changes and metadata. A persistent high residual between geographically nearby endpoints may indicate policy detour, weak local peering, access-network inflation, or location error. A low residual between distant endpoints may reveal a direct cable corridor or unusually efficient peering. The model proposes explanations with confidence; it does not convert correlation into a claim about an unobserved cable.

5.3 Network tomography

Where end-to-end paths share inferred links, delay tomography estimates latent link contributions. In its simplest form, observed path measurements y are related to link-level quantities x through a routing incidence matrix A, a problem introduced as network tomography by Vardi [12].

y(t) = A(t)x(t) + ε(t)    subject to    x(t) ≥ 0
Eq. 5

Regularisation, identifiability analysis, and posterior uncertainty are mandatory. Shared-path inference is too incomplete to support a single deterministic link-delay answer.

5.4 Clustering and persistent topology

Clusters are computed independently in geographic, latency, path, and joint feature spaces, then compared with adjusted mutual information and variation of information. Consensus clusters reveal stable Internet regions; disagreements are more interesting than agreement. A city split across several latency communities may be an interconnection boundary disguised by one geographic label.

Persistent homology avoids pretending that one nearest-neighbour threshold is natural. As the latency threshold ε grows, a Vietoris–Rips complex records when components merge and when cycles appear or disappear. Long-lived features are candidates for genuine multi-scale structure; short-lived features may be noise. Time-windowed persistence summaries can distinguish network states and expose degradation before a conventional scalar threshold fires [14].

5.5 Temporal anomaly detection

Each pair produces a residual time series after subtracting seasonal baseline, endpoint effects, and global load. Robust changepoint methods identify level and variance shifts. An event becomes a network anomaly only when residuals are coherent across a graph neighbourhood, route family, cable corridor, or latent region. This prevents one failing probe from appearing as a continental fracture.

06

Dimensional sweep

How shape changes with dimension

Dimension is not selected by aesthetic preference. Each added dimension must purchase measurable predictive accuracy, neighbourhood stability, or explanatory structure.

Every embedding is fit for d = 1…10 with repeated initialisations and bootstrap samples. Pairwise measurements are split into train and test sets while ensuring every node retains landmark connectivity. Evaluation uses held-out distance error, rank correlation, trustworthiness, continuity, local neighbour recall, and Procrustes-aligned bootstrap stability. An eigenvalue elbow is reported but never used alone.

1D

Order and backbone

A line or ring asks whether the network has a dominant corridor. It exposes centre–edge structure but forces branches to overlap.

2D

The public map

Readable and comparable. Regional clusters emerge, but crossings and false neighbours reveal the price of flattening.

3D

Folds become separations

Some apparent 2D overlaps unfold. Rotation helps inspection but makes static communication more difficult.

5–10D

Prediction space

Coordinates become analytical rather than pictorial. Additional axes are accepted only while held-out error improves stably.

ℍd

Hierarchy as geometry

Negative curvature gives exponentially more room away from the centre, matching tree-like growth and heterogeneous degree.

+t

A moving object

Aligned windows form trajectories. The question becomes whether the Internet drifts, breathes, tears, or snaps between states.

6.1 Local dimension

A global dimensionality estimate can hide different regimes. A densely peered metro may be close to two-dimensional; a region depending on a few international gateways may look tree-like; a multi-homed global network may require more coordinates. Local PCA, neighbourhood reconstruction error, and correlation dimension are estimated around each node. The atlas visualises dimension as a field rather than a verdict.

6.2 Euclidean versus hyperbolic space

Hyperbolic space expands exponentially with radius, allowing hierarchical networks to be embedded with less crowding. Prior work mapped AS topology into a hyperbolic disk and found that geometric proximity could support efficient greedy routing [10]. This study repeats the comparison on contemporary data and asks a narrower question: which relations are preserved better by negative curvature—links, observed paths, latency ranks, or community structure?

6.3 Time as geometry

Independent embeddings cannot be animated directly because translation, rotation, reflection, and scale are arbitrary. Successive windows are aligned with shared anchors and regularised for temporal continuity, while still permitting abrupt movement when supported by held-out observations. The result is a sequence of geometries with uncertainty, not a cosmetic tween between maps.

07

Atlas

Visualisation programme

Visualisation is part of the analysis because each view makes a different failure visible. Every interactive view must expose its measurement window, protocol, sample coverage, uncertainty, distortion, and transformation history. A point with uncertain location should look uncertain; an inferred edge should not resemble an observed traceroute hop.

V1

Three-space comparator

Linked geographic, network, and latency views. Selecting a pair highlights great-circle separation, observed paths, AS sequence, latent distance, inflation, and rank in each space.

V2

Dimensional morph

A controlled transition from 1D through 3D, followed by diagnostic projections of higher-dimensional and hyperbolic fits. A stress panel shows which relationships improve and which remain impossible.

V3

Latency weather

Time-lapse residual fields after geographic and endpoint baselines are removed. Coherent movement suggests events; isolated flicker suggests probe noise.

V4

Triangle-violation atlas

For each pair, reveal intermediaries that produce a lower measured overlay delay. Compare the direct and detour AS paths without implying that the detour is operationally or commercially valid.

V5

Internet contour map

Iso-latency bands around a selected node. Disconnected islands and narrow corridors show where network space folds away from geography.

V6

Persistent topology barcode

Components and cycles across latency thresholds, compared through time. Long-lived features receive inspection; short-lived features remain visible as uncertainty rather than decoration.

Illustrative test / not a result

Sydney ↔ Singapore

Geography suggests regional proximity relative to Europe or North America. The measurement asks whether network-space agrees. If observed routes detour through another hub, the pair may separate in latency space; if a direct cable and favourable peering dominate, they may become close. Both outcomes are informative. The visualisation must show which cables or facilities are documented, which paths are observed, and which explanation remains inferred.

Scientific discipline

Validation and falsification

A compelling map is easy to produce. The difficult part is establishing what it predicts, what it preserves, and what evidence would make us reject it.

01

Withheld pairs

Hold out measured edges, predict their baseline and rank, and report absolute and relative error by region and network class.

02

Withheld nodes

Position unseen probes from landmarks, then evaluate neighbour retrieval and latency prediction. This tests whether coordinates generalise.

03

Bootstrap stability

Resample probes, packets, and windows. Align embeddings and show confidence ellipses, neighbour churn, and cluster persistence.

04

Independent infrastructure

Compare RIPE-derived topology with CAIDA traces and public routing data without allowing one source to validate itself.

05

Event backtesting

Test whether known outages or route changes appear as coherent anomalies using only information available at the time.

06

Negative controls

Shuffle timestamps, rewire graph edges while preserving degree, and randomise labels. Attractive structure that survives inappropriate controls is suspect.

Rejection criteria

A low-dimensional Euclidean model is rejected as a predictive representation if additional dimensions fail to reduce held-out error consistently, local neighbour recall remains below the registered threshold, or bootstrap neighbourhoods are unstable. A hyperbolic model is not preferred merely because it looks hierarchical; it must improve link prediction or path preservation at comparable complexity. A claimed temporal event must exceed probe-level noise and appear across related paths or independent measurement sources.

The final atlas publishes failures. Nodes that cannot be embedded reliably, regions with sparse coverage, pairs with persistent asymmetry, and triangles that contradict the selected metric are not removed from the primary view. They are where the model is learning that the Internet has a different shape than expected.

Boundaries

Ethics, limitations, and outputs

9.1 Ethical and operational measurement

Active measurement consumes shared infrastructure and can resemble unwanted scanning. The campaign uses established platforms, documented probe types, conservative packet rates, public measurement descriptions, contact and opt-out information, and the acceptable-use requirements of each dataset. It does not probe private address space, evade filters, or infer individual behaviour.

Probe locations are published only at the precision provided by the source and appropriate to the analysis. Residential endpoints receive heightened aggregation. The public atlas focuses on cities, ASNs, facilities, and inferred regions—not household-level connectivity. Raw IP addresses, where licensing permits research use, need not appear in the public release.

9.2 Structural limitations

The vantage-point population is not the Internet population. RIPE Atlas and CAIDA provide unusually valuable distributed views, but they overrepresent people and organisations willing and able to host measurement systems. ICMP handling differs from application traffic. Traceroute misses silent hops, encounters load balancing, and cannot directly reveal every router, tunnel, facility, or commercial relationship. Geolocation and alias resolution introduce additional uncertainty.

Latency is workload-dependent. A low-quantile RTT estimates a path floor, not user experience. Median RTT includes congestion but not application processing. One-way delay would better expose directionality but requires clock discipline unavailable across much of the panel. The study must not turn a well-measured subset of global connectivity into a claim about universal Internet quality.

9.3 Reproducible outputs

The project releases a versioned measurement manifest, probe-selection algorithm, immutable source snapshots where licences permit, transformation lineage, analysis code, environment lockfile, and generated figure specifications. Restricted CAIDA data are referenced through required dataset citations and retrieval instructions rather than redistributed [3].

Every published embedding includes its objective, dimension, geometry, random seed, training pairs, held-out pairs, weights, alignment method, and error summaries. The interactive atlas can export the evidence behind any selected point or edge. A visualisation without its provenance is treated as an illustration, not a result.

10 / Conclusion

The Internet is not shaped like the world. It is shaped like the agreements, cables, queues, failures, and measurements through which the world reaches itself.

Asking for the shape of the Internet is useful because the question refuses to decide in advance what counts as distance. The geographic map remains necessary, but it becomes one coordinate system among several. Routing reveals administrative structure. Latency reveals experienced cost. Hyperbolic and graph models reveal hierarchy. Topology reveals features that survive scale. Time reveals that none of these objects holds still.

The aim is not to replace one misleading map with a more sophisticated one. It is to construct an atlas in which models compete, uncertainty remains visible, and contradictions become findings. If Sydney and Singapore appear far apart in one space and close in another, the gap between those pictures is not an inconvenience to smooth away. It is the subject.
