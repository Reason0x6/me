A mutual-authentication architecture for confirming that both parties on a government call are genuine—with one explicit acceptance on each side.

Prepared forServices Australia and Australian Government service-delivery agencies
Prepared byGavin Austin
StatusUnsolicited discussion draft

Executive summary

A phone call should carry conversation—not the burden of proving who is speaking.

Government voice contact has a symmetric trust failure. A citizen cannot infer that a caller is a genuine officer from a withheld number, a displayed name or a convincing voice. An officer cannot infer that a caller is the genuine customer from personal details that may already exist in breach data. Asking either party to disclose another secret over the same untrusted line compounds the problem.

This paper proposes a mutual verification layer around the call. A short-lived agency session is displayed independently in an authenticated citizen app and an authorised officer console. The citizen explicitly accepts that session after local user verification; the officer sees the acceptance and its assurance level. Both surfaces display a human-readable verifier so the people can confirm that they are discussing the same session. No date of birth, account password, login code or verbal password becomes an authenticator.

The short verifier is deliberately not treated as a secret or as transaction approval. It detects mismatched or stale sessions, but a determined attacker can relay it between two simultaneous calls. Relay-resistant handling therefore requires an app-initiated callback or a separate in-app confirmation that displays the exact sensitive action. The architecture authenticates the contact; it does not turn every statement made during the contact into an authorised instruction.

Australia already has much of the foundation. The myGov app supports PIN or device biometric sign-in and push notifications [7]; myID supports Standard and Strong identity strengths and can connect to myGov [8][9]. The ATO’s 2026 Verify Call capability demonstrates one-way, in-app confirmation of outbound calls [10]. The proposal extends that pattern to explicit customer acceptance, mutual status, purpose binding and transaction-level step-up.

01

The problem

The bilateral identity gap

Both people are being asked to make a trust decision with evidence that an attacker can imitate.

Australians reported $2.18 billion in combined scam losses during 2025, a 7.8 per cent increase over 2024 [1]. Services Australia records persistent impersonation of myGov, Medicare and Centrelink, while its own public guidance notes that legitimate outbound calls may arrive from private numbers [6]. This is a structurally poor authentication surface: the signal used by a genuine service is indistinguishable from one an impostor can reproduce.

Citizen → officer

“Are you really from the agency?”

Withheld calling numbers, spoofable labels, urgency and institutional language provide no cryptographic proof of the caller’s authority.

Officer → citizen

“Are you really the customer?”

Names, dates, addresses and reference numbers are identifiers. Once disclosed elsewhere, they are weak authenticators.

Shared channel

“Tell me another secret.”

A password, OTP or answer recited into an untrusted call can be captured, phished or relayed. The channel cannot bootstrap its own trust.

Scope

This paper addresses scheduled and active voice contact between a government service-delivery officer and an enrolled customer. It covers both agency-initiated and citizen-initiated calls. It does not propose identity from voice biometrics, trust from caller ID, or replacement of face-to-face and assisted-service channels.

02

Rules that cannot be negotiated away

Design invariants

I–01

The voice path is hostile

No number, caller label, accent, staff name or spoken value establishes identity.

I–02

Authentication is bilateral

The citizen verifies agency authority; the agency verifies an explicit citizen acceptance.

I–03

Every contact is fresh

Each session has a unique identifier, nonce, purpose, issue time, expiry and terminal state.

I–04

Nothing recitable is a credential

The human verifier may be spoken because it is diagnostic, never sufficient for account access or approval.

I–05

Contact ≠ transaction

Verifying a caller does not authorise a payment, disclosure, destination change or account action.

I–06

Absence must be legible

The app states plainly when no valid agency session exists. Silence or a loading failure is not confirmation.

I–07

Purpose is bound

A verified review call cannot silently become authority for a different or higher-risk action.

I–08

Fallback preserves both sides

Accessibility and non-app paths must not collapse back to one-way knowledge questions.

Trust the authenticated surfaces. Treat the call as an untrusted conversation between them.
03

Logical model

Trust architecture

Four trust anchors replace one ambiguous phone line.

The citizen app authenticates an enrolled account and captures explicit acceptance. The workforce identity service authenticates the officer, while case authorisation confirms that the officer may contact this customer for this purpose. The session service joins those decisions in a signed, short-lived object. The app and console enforce what each side may see or do.

04

Normative flow

Protocol state machine

Verification is a state transition, not a reassuring badge.

The following is the minimum outbound flow. An inbound call uses the same states after account lookup: lookup locates a candidate record but does not authenticate the caller; only the app acceptance changes the customer state to verified.

Created→Offered→Accepted→Active→Closed Any live state → Revoked / Expired
  1. 01

    Authorise the contact

    The console checks workforce identity, role, case access and permitted purpose. The officer cannot mint a session merely by knowing a customer identifier.

    OFFICER PROOF
  2. 02

    Mint a one-use session

    The service creates the signed claims, a high-entropy session identifier and a short human verifier derived for this session. Push content is generic and carries no sign-in link.

    FRESHNESS
  3. 03

    Open independently

    The citizen opens the installed app directly, authenticates, and sees agency, direction, time window, public-facing officer role and purpose. If no session exists, the app says so explicitly.

    AGENCY PROOF
  4. 04

    Accept explicitly

    The citizen reviews “Services Australia is calling now about [purpose]” and selects Accept. The authenticated app signs or submits the nonce; passive receipt of a push is never enough.

    CITIZEN PROOF
  5. 05

    Compare the verifier

    Both surfaces display the same six digits or three words. Either party can read it; the other confirms. A mismatch, expiry or duplicate attempt terminates verification.

    CONVERSATION BINDING
  6. 06

    Step up by action

    A sensitive act creates a new challenge whose app screen names the action and material details. The citizen approves or rejects there—not by saying “yes” on the call.

    TRANSACTION BINDING
  7. 07

    Close and audit

    The session becomes terminal at hang-up, expiry, rejection or revocation. The log retains outcome and policy evidence, not unnecessary conversational content.

    ACCOUNTABILITY
Critical limitation

Live relay is not solved by matching a code.

An attacker can place one call to the citizen and another to the officer, relay the displayed verifier in real time, and tell each victim that it matches. The verifier blocks guessing, stale reuse and accidental session confusion; it is not distance bounding and it does not cryptographically identify a PSTN audio path.

For higher assurance

Use an app-initiated callback routed to the session, or move the consequential approval into an independently displayed, device-authorised transaction. Never allow the verified-call flag alone to approve a sensitive action.

Assumptions

The design assumes the agency signing service, citizen identity session and workforce identity system retain their stated assurance. It does not protect a device already controlled by malware, a fully compromised agency account acting within granted authority, or a person being coerced while they approve an accurately described action. Those risks require endpoint, insider-risk and safeguarding controls around the protocol.

Usable security

Acceptance and inclusion

The secure path should be the shortest path, but never the only path.

The citizen’s ordinary case is one independent action: open the app and accept the described contact. The officer’s ordinary case is observational: the console moves from unverified to verified at [assurance level]. Matching the verifier is a conversation check, not a second credential ceremony.

Acceptance criteria

A1

No spoken identity quiz

Name, date of birth, address and reference numbers may support search or recovery but never satisfy the verified state.

A2

No notification link

Notifications say to open the app. The complete session is visible only after authentication.

A3

No coercive approval

The app never asks a user to transfer funds, disclose a login code or approve an action merely to keep a call connected.

A4

No silent purpose drift

A higher-risk subject requires a new explicit screen; the voice conversation cannot broaden the session.

A5

No inaccessible security

WCAG conformance, screen-reader labels, large text, plain language, language support and adequate time are release gates.

A6

No digital-only service

App-initiated callback, published-number callback, supported service-centre and nominee pathways remain available.

Fallback principle

Graceful degradation must degrade convenience before it degrades trust.

A link-free SMS can notify or provide a non-sensitive locator, but possession of a phone number is not equivalent to authenticated identity. For a person without the app, the safer fallback is a citizen-initiated call to a independently sourced official number, routed by an opaque session reference, followed by the agency’s established assisted-verification policy. High-risk actions should move to an accessible service centre or other stronger channel rather than silently reverting to reusable questions.

07

Australian context

Policy alignment and precedent

01 / Confirmed

SMS Sender ID Register

Since 1 July 2026, unregistered branded SMS/MMS sender IDs are labelled “Unverified.” ACMA is explicit that these rules concern messages, not live voice calls [2][3].

02 / In force

Scams Prevention Framework

The Act requires regulated entities to take reasonable steps to prevent, detect, report, disrupt and respond to scams [4]. Government is not made a regulated sector by this proposal; adopting the pattern would be leadership by example.

03 / Existing rail

myGov and myID

myGov already supplies authenticated app access and notifications. myID can provide higher identity strength where service policy requires it. This paper does not claim current apps expose every cryptographic primitive needed by the proposed flow.

04 / Direct precedent

ATO Verify Call

The ATO introduced an app workflow in which a taxpayer requests verification and the officer confirms through an internal tool within 30 seconds [10]. That validates the channel-separation pattern in Australian government.

What is proposed beyond the precedent

The extension is not new cryptography. It is a product and assurance model: explicit citizen acceptance visible to the officer; signed purpose and direction; a mutual human verifier; declared customer assurance; terminal session state; privacy-minimised audit; a relay-aware strong callback mode; and transaction-specific step-up. It should be evaluated against the ATO implementation before creating parallel infrastructure.

08

Delivery proposal

Pilot and measures

Begin with a narrow outbound contact where the harm, volume and current verification cost are measurable.

A discovery phase should compare three options before bespoke implementation: reuse or federation with the ATO Verify Call service; extension of common myGov capabilities; or a Services Australia-specific session service built to a shared government profile. The pilot should not begin by selecting cryptographic formats.

01

Discovery

Threat modelling with fraud, service delivery, identity, privacy, accessibility, workforce and citizen representatives. Confirm legal basis and current platform capabilities.

02

Prototype

Clickable app and console flows, then a non-production signed-session service. Test negative states, rejection, expiry, recovery and relay warnings—not only the happy path.

03

Limited pilot

One outbound contact type, opt-in cohort, trained officers, safe fallback and independent security testing. No sensitive action relies solely on the call flag.

04

Decision

Publish outcome measures and equity effects. Expand only if assurance improves without shifting unacceptable burden onto excluded cohorts or staff.

Stop conditions

Pause the pilot if staff or citizens treat the verifier as a secret; if users approve without understanding the displayed purpose; if real-time relay testing succeeds in authorising a sensitive action; if a fallback cohort experiences materially worse service; or if session metadata exposes more information than the present process.

Appendix A

Technical profile

Identity

Citizen

Authenticated myGov account; declared identity-strength claim where needed; proposed platform-credential assertion or equivalent fresh app proof; recovery kept separate from routine verification.

Identity

Officer

Phishing-resistant workforce authentication, managed device posture, case and purpose authorisation, least privilege and rapid session revocation.

Service

Session

Policy engine, high-entropy identifier, one-use nonce, signed claims, short TTL, explicit state machine, replay cache, rate limits and idempotent closure.

Clients

Enforcement

App and console validate issuer, audience, signature, time, nonce, purpose and current server state. A valid signature on an expired or revoked object is not acceptance.

Transport

Network

Current supported TLS with normal platform trust and strong service authentication. Certificate pinning is optional and should be justified against rotation and outage risk.

Operations

Audit

Append-only security events for issuance, delivery, acceptance, rejection, expiry, revocation and step-up outcome; strict retention, role access and anomaly detection.

Verifier construction

The session identifier must be high entropy and never shortened for human use. A separate verifier can be derived from the session transcript with a domain-separated keyed construction and encoded as, for example, three unambiguous words or six digits. Its collision and online-guessing properties must be analysed with rate limits and the very short session window. Because it is spoken, the verifier is public after use and contributes no reusable credential strength.

State and concurrency

Only one active high-assurance contact session should exist for the same customer and purpose unless the UI makes concurrency unmistakable. Acceptance uses compare-and-set semantics against the offered state and nonce. Closing, rejecting, expiring or revoking the session is irreversible. A second acceptance returns the existing terminal result without replaying an action.

Privacy profile

Push notifications contain no sensitive reason or customer data. Pairwise identifiers replace raw customer numbers outside the core case system. Citizen displays are gated behind authentication. Officer identity presentation follows safety policy. Analytics use aggregation and suppression, and security-log retention is justified independently from business-record retention.

Non-functional priorities

WCAG accessibility, low-bandwidth behaviour, notification-independent discovery, clock-skew handling, degraded-mode clarity, key rotation, multi-device enrolment, device loss, account recovery, service availability, disaster recovery and full red-team exercises are protocol requirements—not later implementation details.

10 / Conclusion

Move proof out of the conversation.

A genuine government officer should not need to sound trustworthy. A genuine citizen should not need to know facts that a criminal cannot learn. Both should be able to look at a surface they reached independently and see that the same authorised, short-lived contact exists.

The simplest form is deliberately modest: issue, open, accept, compare, expire. Its strength comes from refusing to overstate what each step proves. The app proves the agency session. Explicit acceptance proves control of the authenticated citizen channel at that moment. The verifier exposes mismatch. None of those alone authorises a sensitive transaction.

Australia’s ATO has already demonstrated the central product insight: the burden of proving a call can move into an authenticated app. The next step is to make that assurance mutual, purpose-bound, accessible and relay-aware across government services.
